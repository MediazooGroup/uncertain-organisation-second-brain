## The story

### The need
People on the pilot kept asking what it could spend and what they could put into Claude, and nobody had one place to look (C1). The answers sat in different documents, with no clear sense of which to trust (C2).

We wanted to find out whether someone on the pilot can find the answer to either question and see where it came from (C14).

### What we did
We ran a small learning pilot with twelve people. Nine of the twelve came to the first workshop, on 8 February 2027 (C3).

The pilot's rules on what it may use were written down, in a note owned by Ruth Evans, information governance adviser (C5).

The pilot's budget was written down too. It is £8,000, approved by Daniel Reed, operations manager, on 5 February 2027 for this small pilot only. It replaced a £10,000 planning figure that Maya Patel wrote on 2 February 2027, which was her own estimate and never an approval (C13).

One real question was traced back to its source. A service coordinator asked who to ask before putting a client file into Claude, and the pilot's notes answered: Ruth Evans, from that note. That was one question only (C6).

### What came of it
Six of the nine who came told Maya Patel, who coordinates the pilot, that they used a skill again the following week (C4). That is what they said. Nobody was timed or checked.

Leila Ahmed, communications specialist, described checking sources this way: "I can see where I need to step in now." (C7) She was speaking about her own experience, for one kind of task.

One service coordinator gave two small accounts. A draft action list gave a task to the wrong one of two people who share a first name (C9). The instruction was then changed to ask for a full name or a role (C10). It has not been tried again, so we do not know yet whether the change works.

The same service coordinator estimates that a weekly update used to take about 75 minutes, and that a later run took about 50, including checking (C8). Whether the 75 included checking is Unknown. Both are their own estimates, for one task, and nobody timed either run. We do not know whether it was the same task both times, or how many runs the figures cover. We are not saying the pilot made work quicker, and the figures say nothing about the rest of the team.

### What it means
A small pilot taught us where people need to step in. It is a story about learning, not about proof. It describes these twelve people and nobody else.

A few people also said they were not sure when to trust an answer and when to check (C11). That question is still open.

### What a colleague could try first
Try one task using only material the pilot is allowed to use: its own planning notes, approved decisions, process notes, or made-up figures for practice. Then check where the answer came from. If you are not sure whether something is allowed, ask before you paste it in. (C5)

These are the pilot's own rules, in a note owned by Ruth Evans, information governance adviser. Maya Patel confirmed this with her on 15 February 2027. The rules have not been checked for other teams.

### The next step
The pilot plans one more small trial. It is meant to find out whether people outside the pilot can try one task they are allowed to use and check where the answer came from (C12). It builds on the nine of twelve who came and the six of nine who used a skill again (C3, C4), which describe this pilot only. Its size, dates and people are Unknown.

Whether to roll out stays open, and Unknown. It needs Ruth Evans's check of what data may be shared, and a decision from someone with the right to say so. Who decides on roll-out is Unknown.
