# Try one task and check it: what our small pilot taught us

For: a team leader outside the pilot who wants to know what they can try.
Read: as a page, on a phone or laptop, in the pilot team's look.
Master story used: 2027-03-22.
By: Maya Patel, L&D lead, with Leila Ahmed, communications specialist. 29 March 2027.

If you lead a team outside our pilot, here is the one thing we would ask you to try. Pick one task. Use only material you are allowed to use. Then check where the answer came from. We would ask that a person does the checking.

## Try this first

Pick one task. Use only material the pilot is allowed to use: its own planning notes, approved decisions, process notes, or made-up figures for practice.

Then check where the answer came from.

If you are not sure whether something is allowed, ask before you paste it in. See below for the one example we have of who the pilot's notes named.

## What "allowed" means here

These are the pilot's own rules. They are written down in a note owned by Ruth Evans, information governance adviser, and Maya Patel confirmed them with her on 15 February 2027. They have not been checked for other teams. That is why we say "allowed" and not "safe".

One example of the notes at work: a service coordinator asked who to ask before putting a client file into Claude, and the pilot's notes answered Ruth Evans. That was one question only.

## Why we started

People on the pilot kept asking what it could spend and what they could put into Claude, and nobody had one place to look. As Maya Patel, who coordinates the pilot, describes it, the answers sat in different documents, and it was not clear which to trust.

So we ran a small learning pilot with twelve people.

## What came of it

9 of 12 people invited came to the first workshop, on 8 February 2027. This describes our pilot only.

6 of 9 of those who came told Maya Patel they used a skill again the following week. That is what they said. Nobody was timed or checked.

Leila Ahmed described what she noticed when checking sources:

> "I can see where I need to step in now."
> Leila Ahmed, communications specialist. Her own experience, for one kind of task. It is not a measure.

### Two small accounts from one service coordinator

They reported that a draft action list gave a task to the wrong one of two people who share a first name. The instruction was then changed to ask for a full name or a role. It has not been tried again, so we do not know yet whether the change works.

The same service coordinator estimates that a weekly update used to take about 75 minutes, and that a later run took about 50, including checking. Both are their own estimates for one task, and nobody timed either run. We do not know whether it was the same task both times, how many runs the figures cover, or whether the 75 minutes included checking. We are not saying the pilot made work quicker, and the figures say nothing about the rest of the team.

## What this means

A small pilot taught us where people need to step in. It is a story about learning, not about proof. It describes these twelve people and nobody else.

So we would ask that a person does the checking. A few people also told us they were not sure when to trust an answer and when to check. That question is still open.

## What we plan next

We plan one more small trial. It is meant to find out whether people outside the pilot can try one task they are allowed to use and check where the answer came from. It builds on the 9 of 12 and the 6 of 9 above, which describe this pilot only. We do not know yet its size, its dates or who will take part.

Whether to roll out is still open. It needs Ruth Evans to check what data may be shared, and a decision from someone with the right to say so. We do not know yet who that is.

## What we do not know yet

- Why three of the twelve did not come to the first workshop. Nobody has asked them.
- What the six used a skill for, and whether the material was allowed.
- When to trust an answer and when to check.
- Whether the changed instruction works.
- Whether to roll out, and who decides.

Try one task. Check where the answer came from. Ask before you paste in anything you are not sure about.
